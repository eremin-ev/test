\section{Angular momentum theorem from Newton’s laws}

It follows from Newton’s second law applied to every particle of a rigid body,
plus the rigid-body velocity constraint. The key point is that $I$ is
constant only in the \emph{body-fixed frame}, so when you take the inertial time
derivative of \(\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega\), a rotating-frame term appears.
This means differentiate with respect to time while holding the inertial-frame basis
vectors fixed.

For each particle $i$, Newton's second law in inertial frame is

\[
m_i \ddot{\mathbf R}_i=\mathbf F_i \qquad \mathbf R_i=\mathbf r_i + \mathbf O
\]

where $\mathbf R_i$ --- \emph{inertial} position vector of particle $i$ relative to some fixed origin,
$\mathbf O = \mathbf O(t)$ --- reference origin (often an inertial-frame origin, but not necessary)
relative to the same fixed origin,
$\mathbf r_i$ --- relative vector position of a particle $i$, not necessarily inertial.
Substitute $\mathbf R_i$

\[
m_i \ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i - m_i \ddot{\mathbf O}.
\]

Let be $\mathbf O = (0, 0)$ for simplicity.  So $\ddot{\mathbf O} = \mathbf 0,$
$\mathbf r_i$ is inertial, and

\[
m_i \ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i.
\]

Cross with \(\mathbf r_i\) and sum over all particles:

\begin{equation}
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times \mathbf F_i .
\label{eq:newton_cross}
\end{equation}

Define the total angular momentum about the chosen origin $\mathbf O$ as

\[
\mathbf L_O
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times \mathbf p_i,
\qquad
\mathbf p_i
=
m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i.
\]

Now differentiate \(\mathbf L_O\) with respect to time. Since \(m_i\) is constant,

\[
\frac{d\mathbf L_O}{dt}
=
\sum_i \frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf r_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\right).
\]

Use the product rule for a cross product:

\[
\left[
\quad
\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)
=
\dot{\mathbf a}\cdot\mathbf b+\mathbf a\cdot\dot{\mathbf b},
\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)
=
\dot{\mathbf a}\times\mathbf b+\mathbf a\times\dot{\mathbf b}
\quad
\right],
\]

\[
\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf r_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i)
=
\dot{\mathbf r}_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i
+
\mathbf r_i\times m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i.
\]

The first term vanishes because a vector crossed with itself is
zero (\(\mathbf a\times\mathbf a=0\)):

\[
\dot{\mathbf r}_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i
=
m_i(\dot{\mathbf r}_i\times \dot{\mathbf r}_i)
=
0.
\]

Therefore, the left hand side of \eqref{eq:newton_cross} is

\[
\frac{d\mathbf L_O}{dt}
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i.
\]

Define the sum of moments of all forces about the chosen origin $\mathbf O$ as
total torque:
\[
\boldsymbol\tau
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times \mathbf F_i.
\]

%The right-hand side of \eqref{eq:newton_cross} is :

Now split the force acting on particle $i$ into external and internal:
\[
\mathbf F_i = \mathbf F_i^{\rm ext} + \mathbf F_i^{\rm int}
\]
so
\[
\boldsymbol\tau
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i \times \mathbf F_i
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i \times \mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}
+
\sum_i \mathbf r_i \times \mathbf F_i^{\rm int}
=
\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm ext} + \boldsymbol\tau_{\rm int}
\]

\hrule

For an ideal rigid body, the internal-force term cancels in pairs.  Let
\[
\mathbf F_{ij}
\]
denote the force on particle \(i\) due to particle \(j\).
Then the total internal force on particle \(i\) is
\[
\mathbf F_i^{\rm int}=\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}.
\]

The total internal force on the whole system is therefore
\[
\mathbf F^{\rm int}
=
\sum_i \mathbf F_i^{\rm int}
=
\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}.
\]

Now use Newton’s third law in its pairwise form:
\[
\mathbf F_{ij}=-\mathbf F_{ji}, \qquad i\ne j.
\]

The double sum contains both \(\mathbf F_{ij}\) and \(\mathbf F_{ji}\) for
every unordered pair $\{i,j\}$.

\[
\left(\quad
\begin{minipage}{0.8\textwidth}
The convention is:
\begin{itemize}
\item \((i,j)\) denotes an ordered pair ($(i,j) \ne (j,i)$)
\item \(\{i,j\}\) denotes an unordered pair, or a set ($\{i,j\}$ and $\{j,i)$) are the same sets)
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\quad\right)
\]

So group the terms by unordered pairs $\{i,j\}$ where $i<j$
(using double-sum regrouping identity).  Split the ordered pairs $(i,j),$
\(i\ne j\), into two classes:
\[
i<j \qquad\text{and}\qquad i>j.
\]

Thus

\[
\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}
=
\sum_{i<j}\mathbf F_{ij}
+
\sum_{i>j}\mathbf F_{ij}.
\]

There are $n(n-1)$ ordered pairs $(i,j)$ where $i \ne j$ on the left.

There are $\binom n 2=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ unordered pairs $\{i,j\}$ where $i<j$,
and the same amount of pairs where $i>j$ on the right.  So the number of terms matches:
\[
\underbrace{\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}}_{n(n-1)}
=
\underbrace{\sum_{i<j}\mathbf F_{ij}}_{\frac{n(n-1)}2}
+
\underbrace{\sum_{i>j}\mathbf F_{ij}}_{\frac{n(n-1)}2}.
\]

The two sums are disjoint because no pair $(i,j)$ can satisfy both $i<j$
and $i>j$. Together they cover exactly all ordered pairs with $i \ne j$.

The second sum:

\[
\sum_{i>j}\mathbf F_{ij}.
\]

The map $(i,j) \mapsto (j,i)$ is a bijection from the set of pairs with $i>j$ to
the set of pairs with \(i<j\). Therefore, by renaming dummy indices,

\[
\sum_{i>j}\mathbf a_{ij}
=
\sum_{i<j}\mathbf a_{ji}.
\]

Hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
S
&=
\sum_{i<j}\mathbf a_{ij}
+
\sum_{i<j}\mathbf a_{ji} \\
&=
\boxed{
\sum_{i<j}\left(\mathbf a_{ij}+\mathbf a_{ji}\right).
}
\end{aligned}
\]

This proves the identity.

=

\left(

So “internal forces cancel in pairs” means precisely this: in the **sum over all particles**, every internal force has an equal-and-opposite partner, and the pair cancels.

Two important qualifications:

1. This proves cancellation of the **resultant internal force**. It does not say \(\mathbf F_{ij}=0\) individually. Individual internal forces are generally nonzero; they just cancel pairwise in the total sum.

2. For the **internal torque** to cancel, Newton’s third law in the simple form \(\mathbf F_{ij}=-\mathbf F_{ji}\) is not enough. One also needs the internal forces to be central, i.e.

\[
\mathbf F_{ij}\parallel \mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j,
\]

or one needs an equivalent ideal-constraint assumption for the rigid body. Then

\[
\boldsymbol\tau^{\rm int}
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times \mathbf F_i^{\rm int}
=
\sum_{i<j}
(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j)\times \mathbf F_{ij}
=
0.
\]

So the standard derivation uses:

- Newton’s third law \(\mathbf F_{ij}=-\mathbf F_{ji}\) to kill the resultant internal force;
- central internal forces, or ideal rigid constraints, to kill the resultant internal torque.

Without those assumptions, the cancellation is not guaranteed.




Standard
assumption: internal forces are equal and opposite along the line joining the
particles:

\[
  \boldsymbol\tau_{\mathrm{int}}
  = \sum_i \mathbf r_i \times \mathbf F_i^{\mathrm{int}}
  = \sum_{i<j} (\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j)\times \mathbf F_{ij}
  = \mathbf 0,
\]

because internal forces are equal and opposite and act along the line
joining the particles (strong form of Newton's third law).



\hrule

So

\[
\boldsymbol\tau
=
\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm ext} + \boldsymbol\tau_{\rm int}
=
\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm ext} + \mathbf 0
=
\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm ext}.
\]

Thus the step gives the angular momentum theorem:

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{inertial}}
=
\boldsymbol\tau.
\label{eq:angular_momentum}
\end{equation}

This is often called Euler’s second law, but it is derived from Newton’s laws.

\section{Rigid-body angular momentum \(\mathbf L\)}

Let the centre of mass be

\[
\mathbf R=\frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i,
\qquad M=\sum_i m_i,
\]

and define the position of particle \(i\) relative to the centre of mass as

\[
\boldsymbol\rho_i=\mathbf r_i-\mathbf R.
\]

Then

\[
\mathbf r_i=\mathbf R+\boldsymbol\rho_i,
\qquad
\dot{\mathbf r}_i=\dot{\mathbf R}+\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i.
\]

Define the angular momentum about the origin as

\[
\mathbf L
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i.
\]

Substitute $\mathbf r_i$ and $\dot{\mathbf r}_i$ into the definition

\[
\mathbf L
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i
=
\sum_i (\mathbf R+\boldsymbol\rho_i)\times m_i(\dot{\mathbf R}+\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i).
\]

Expand the cross product (distribute): for each $i$,

\[
(\mathbf R + \boldsymbol\rho_i) \times m_i(\dot{\mathbf R} + \dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i)
=
\mathbf R \times m_i(\dot{\mathbf R} + \dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i)
+
\boldsymbol\rho_i \times m_i(\dot{\mathbf R} + \dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i)=
\]

\[
=
m_i\mathbf R \times \dot{\mathbf R}
+
m_i\mathbf R \times \dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
+
m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i \times \dot{\mathbf R}
+
m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i \times \dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i.
\]

Sum over $i$:

\[
\mathbf L
=
\underbrace{\sum_i m_i\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\mathbf R}}_{(1)}
+\underbrace{\sum_i m_i\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i}_{(2)}
+\underbrace{\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\mathbf R}}_{(3)}
+\underbrace{\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i}_{(4)}.
\]

Evaluate each term.

\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathbf R$ and $\dot{\mathbf R}$ do not depend on \(i\), so they pull out of the sum:
\[
\sum_i m_i\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\mathbf R}
=
\mathbf R\times\dot{\mathbf R}\sum_i m_i
=
M\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\mathbf R}.
\]

\item $\mathbf R$ is independent of $i$, so

\[
\sum_i m_i\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
=
\mathbf R\times\sum_i m_i\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
=
\mathbf R\times\mathbf 0
=
\mathbf 0,
\]

because \(\sum_i m_i\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i=\dfrac{d}{dt}\sum_i m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i=\mathbf 0\) (see below).

\item $\dot{\mathbf R}$ is independent of $i$, so

\[
\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\mathbf R}
=
\left(\sum_i m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i\right)\times\dot{\mathbf R}
=
\mathbf 0\times\dot{\mathbf R}
=
\mathbf 0,
\]

because \(\sum_i m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i=\mathbf 0\) (see below).

\item This is the only term that survives untouched:

\[
\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i.
\]

\end{enumerate}

Why the two vanishing sums are zero?  By definition of the centre of mass,

\[
\sum_i m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i
=
\sum_i m_i(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf R)
=
\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i-M\mathbf R
=
M\mathbf R-M\mathbf R
=
\mathbf 0.
\]

Differentiating with respect to time (masses constant):

\[
\sum_i m_i\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
=
\frac{d}{dt}\sum_i m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i
=
\mathbf 0.
\]

So both \(\sum_i m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i\) and
\(\sum_i m_i\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i\) vanish identically — this is the mathematical
content of "the centre of mass is the mass-weighted mean position."

Putting the four terms together,

\[
\mathbf L
=
M\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\mathbf R}
+\mathbf 0+\mathbf 0
+\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i.
\]

Hence

\[
\mathbf L_{\rm origin}
=
\underbrace{M\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\mathbf R}}_{\mathbf L_{\rm orbital} \text{(CM about origin)}}
+
\underbrace{\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i}_{\mathbf L_{\rm CM}\ \text{(spin about CM)}}
\]

The first term is the angular momentum of the centre of mass about the origin:

\[
\mathbf L_{\rm orbital}
=
M\,\mathbf R\times\dot{\mathbf R}.
\]

The second term is the angular momentum about the centre of mass (CM):

\[
\mathbf L_{\rm CM}
=
\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
\]


and therefore

\[
\mathbf L_{\rm CM}
=
\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
=
\mathbf L_{\rm origin}-\mathbf R\times\mathbf P,
\qquad
\mathbf P=M\dot{\mathbf R}.
\]

\hrulefill

Equivalently,

\[
\mathbf L_{\rm CM}
=
\mathbf L_{\rm origin}-\mathbf R\times \mathbf P,
\qquad
\mathbf P=M\dot{\mathbf R}.
\]

So the formula

\[
\mathbf L=\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
\]

is just the angular momentum about the centre of mass, written using coordinates measured from the centre of mass. In that frame, for a rigid body,

\[
\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i=\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\rho_i,
\]

which gives

\[
\mathbf L
=
\sum_i m_i\,\boldsymbol\rho_i\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\rho_i)
=
I\boldsymbol\omega.
\]

\hrulefill

About the center of mass, write a position of particle \(i\) relative to
the centre of mass

\[
\boldsymbol\rho_i=\mathbf r_i-\mathbf R,
\]

where \(\mathbf R\) is the center of mass. For a rigid body,

\[
\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i=\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\rho_i.
\]

Therefore

\[
\mathbf L
=
\sum_i m_i \boldsymbol\rho_i\times\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}_i
=
\sum_i m_i \boldsymbol\rho_i\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\rho_i).
\]

Using

\[
\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)
=
\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),
\]

we get

\[
\mathbf L
=
\left[
\sum_i m_i\left(|\boldsymbol\rho_i|^2\mathbf 1
-\boldsymbol\rho_i\boldsymbol\rho_i^T\right)
\right]\boldsymbol\omega.
\]

Define the inertia tensor

\[
I=\sum_i m_i\left(|\boldsymbol\rho_i|^2\mathbf 1
-\boldsymbol\rho_i\boldsymbol\rho_i^T\right).
\]

Then

\[
\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega.
\]

In the \emph{body-fixed frame}, the particle coordinates \(\boldsymbol\rho_i\) are
constant, so \(I\) is a constant matrix. In the inertial frame, \(I(t)\) would
generally change with time.

\section{Differentiate \(\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega\) correctly}

The torque equation is inertial:

\[
\boldsymbol\tau=
\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{inertial}}.
\]

For any vector \(\mathbf A\), the derivative in the inertial frame is related
to the derivative in a frame rotating with angular velocity
\(\boldsymbol\omega\) by

\[
\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{inertial}}
=
\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{body}}
+
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf A.
\]

Apply this to \(\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega\). Since \(I\) is constant in the body frame,

\[
\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{body}}
=
I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{inertial}}
=
I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
+
\boldsymbol\omega\times(I\boldsymbol\omega).
\]

Using \(\boldsymbol\tau=d\mathbf L/dt|_{\text{inertial}}\),

\[
\boxed{
\boldsymbol\tau
=
I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
+
\boldsymbol\omega\times(I\boldsymbol\omega)
}
\]

This is the Euler rotation equation.

\section{Principal-axis form}

If the body axes are principal axes, \(I=\operatorname{diag}(I_1,I_2,I_3)\),
and the vector equation becomes the three Euler equations:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tau_1 &= I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3,\\
\tau_2 &= I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1,\\
\tau_3 &= I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2.
\end{aligned}
\]

So the extra \(\boldsymbol\omega\times(I\boldsymbol\omega)\) term is not an
additional physical torque; it is the correction that appears because the
body-fixed basis rotates with the body.

Angular momentum is defined as
\[
\mathbf L=\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times \mathbf p_i
=\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i
\]

because it is the **moment of linear momentum** about the chosen origin. The cross product is not arbitrary: it is exactly the mathematical object that measures “how much momentum is circulating around the origin,” rather than how much is moving radially away from or toward it.

Key points:

1. **Only the tangential component of momentum contributes to rotation.**

   For a particle,
   \[
   |\mathbf r\times \mathbf p|=r p\sin\theta,
   \]
   where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf p\). If \(\mathbf p\parallel \mathbf r\), the particle moves radially and \(\mathbf L=0\). If \(\mathbf p\perp \mathbf r\), the full momentum contributes to rotation. The cross product automatically picks out the perpendicular/tangential part.

2. **The cross product gives the axis and orientation of rotation.**
   \(\mathbf r\times\mathbf p\) is perpendicular to the plane spanned by \(\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf p\), with direction given by the right-hand rule. Thus it is an axial vector: its direction is the rotation axis, and its magnitude measures the rotational “amount.”

3. **It follows from rotational symmetry / torque.**
   For a particle,
   \[
   \dot{\mathbf L}
   =\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf p+\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf p}
   =0+\mathbf r\times\mathbf F
   =\boldsymbol\tau.
   \]
   So the cross-product definition is precisely the one whose time derivative gives torque:
   \[
   \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F.
   \]
   If angular momentum were defined with a dot product, it would not have this rotational meaning or this torque relation.

4. **Why \(m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\)?**
   For an ordinary point mass,
   \[
   \mathbf p_i=m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i
   \]
   is the linear momentum. More generally, \(\mathbf p_i\) should be the momentum conjugate to \(\mathbf r_i\); for simple mechanical systems that is \(m_i\dot{\mathbf r}_i\). Thus angular momentum is the moment of the actual linear momentum.

5. **In rigid-body/Euler equations**, one writes
   \[
   \mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega,
   \]
   and in the body frame
   \[
   \frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{inertial}}
   =
   \frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{body}}
   +\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L
   =\boldsymbol\tau.
   \]
   Hence
   \[
   I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
   +\boldsymbol\omega\times(I\boldsymbol\omega)
   =\boldsymbol\tau,
   \]
   the Euler rotation equation. The cross product appears because angular momentum is a vector associated with rotation and because the body-frame axes themselves rotate.

So the cross product is there because angular momentum is the **oriented moment of momentum about a point**: it measures rotational motion, gives the rotation axis, and is the quantity whose rate of change is torque.

Let
\[
\mathbf r=x\mathbf i+y\mathbf j,\qquad
\mathbf r'=x'\mathbf i+y'\mathbf j,
\]
where \(\mathbf r'\) is \(\mathbf r\) rotated counterclockwise by \(\theta\) in
the orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf i, \mathbf j\}$.

Let \(J\) denote rotation by \(+90^\circ\) counterclockwise. In the fixed basis,
\[
J\mathbf i=\mathbf j,
\qquad
J\mathbf j=-\mathbf i.
\]

(And also
\( J(-\mathbf i)=-J\mathbf i=-\mathbf j, \)
\( J(-\mathbf j)=-J\mathbf j=\mathbf i. \))

Therefore
\[
J\mathbf r=J(x\mathbf i+y\mathbf j)
=x\mathbf j-y\mathbf i
=-y\mathbf i+x\mathbf j.
\]
The vectors \(\mathbf r\) and \(J\mathbf r\) are orthogonal and have the same length:
\[
\mathbf r\cdot J\mathbf r=0, \qquad \|J\mathbf r\|=\|\mathbf r\|.
\]

The decomposition of $\mathbf r'$ onto the orthogonal basis \(\{\mathbf r,J\mathbf r\}\) is
\[
\mathbf r'
=
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf r}(\mathbf r')
+
\operatorname{proj}_{J\mathbf r}(\mathbf r').
\]

\[
\left(\quad
\begin{minipage}{0.8\textwidth}
The vector projection of \(\mathbf a\) onto \(\mathbf b\) is

\[
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}(\mathbf a)
= \frac{\mathbf a\cdot \mathbf b}{\|\mathbf b\|}\frac{\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf b\|}.
\]
Note that
\[
\|\mathbf b\|\,\|\mathbf b\|=\|\mathbf b\|^2=\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b,
\]
so this can be written as
\[
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}(\mathbf a)
= \frac{\mathbf a\cdot \mathbf b}{\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b}\mathbf b.
\]

Equivalently, if \(\mathbf u=\dfrac{\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf b\|}\) is the unit vector in
the direction of \(\mathbf b\), then

\[
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}(\mathbf a)
= (\mathbf a\cdot \mathbf u)\mathbf u.
\]
\end{minipage}
\quad\right)
\]

Using this definition, rewrite
\[
\mathbf r'
=
\frac{\mathbf r'\cdot\mathbf r}{\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r}\,\mathbf r
+
\frac{\mathbf r'\cdot J\mathbf r}{(J\mathbf r)\cdot(J\mathbf r)}\,J\mathbf r.
\]

Since \(\mathbf r'\) is the rotation of \(\mathbf r\) by \(\theta\)
it preserves the length \(\|\mathbf r'\|=\|\mathbf r\|,\) and the angle between
\(\mathbf r'\) and \(\mathbf r\) has cosine \(\cos\theta,\) so by definition
\[
\mathbf r'\cdot \mathbf r
=
\|\mathbf r'\|\,\|\mathbf r\|\cos\theta
=
\|\mathbf r\|^2\cos\theta
=
\left(\mathbf r \cdot \mathbf r\right) \cos\theta.
\]

\(J\mathbf r\) is \(90^\circ\) ahead of \(\mathbf r\) and
\(\mathbf r'\) is \(\theta\) ahead of \(\mathbf r\), thus
the angle between \(\mathbf r'\) and \(J\mathbf r\) is
\[
90^\circ-\theta.
\]

The rotation doesn't change the length of \(J\mathbf r\), so
\(\|\mathbf r'\| = \|J\mathbf r\|,\) and
\[
\mathbf r'\cdot J\mathbf r
=
\|\mathbf r'\|\,\|J\mathbf r\| \cos(90^\circ-\theta)
=
\|J\mathbf r\|^2 \sin\theta
=
(J\mathbf r)\cdot(J\mathbf r) \sin\theta.
\]

These give
\[
\frac{\mathbf r'\cdot \mathbf r}{\mathbf r\cdot \mathbf r}
=
\cos\theta,
\qquad
\frac{\mathbf r'\cdot J\mathbf r}{(J\mathbf r)\cdot(J\mathbf r)}
=
\sin\theta,
\]
so
\[
\mathbf r'=\cos\theta\,\mathbf r+\sin\theta\,J\mathbf r.
\]

Now substitute \(\mathbf r=x\mathbf i+y\mathbf j\) and \(J\mathbf r=-y\mathbf i+x\mathbf j\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf r' &=\cos\theta\,(x\mathbf i+y\mathbf j) +\sin\theta\,(-y\mathbf i+x\mathbf j) \\
           &=(x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta)\mathbf i +(x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta)\mathbf j.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since
\[
\mathbf r'=x'\mathbf i+y'\mathbf j,
\]
we read off
\[
\begin{aligned}
x'&=x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta, \\
y'&=x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{pmatrix}x'\\ y'\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\ y\end{pmatrix}.
\]
or
\[
\mathbf r'
=
R \mathbf r.
\]

Where $R$ is the rotation matrix
\[
R=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Equivalently, using dot products directly from \(\mathbf r'=x'\mathbf i+y'\mathbf j\),
\[
\mathbf r'\cdot\mathbf r
=x'x+y'y
=(x^2+y^2)\cos\theta,
\]
\[
\mathbf r'\cdot J\mathbf r
=-x'y+y'x
=(x^2+y^2)\sin\theta.
\]
Solving these two equations for \(x',y'\) gives the same result.

For a \emph{clockwise} rotation, replace \(\theta\) by \(-\theta\),
which flips the signs of the off-diagonal entries.

\hrule

Let \(A\) be an inertial origin. Write the inertial position of particle \(i\) as
\(\mathbf R_i\), and choose an origin point \(O(t)\) whose position vector is \(\mathbf O(t)\). Put

\[
\mathbf r_i:=\mathbf R_i-\mathbf O .
\]

The equation written in the question,

\[
m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i,
\]

is Newton’s law only if \(\ddot{\mathbf O}=0\), i.e. if \(O\) is an inertial origin
(fixed, or moving uniformly). More generally,

\[
m_i\ddot{\mathbf R}_i=\mathbf F_i
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i-m_i\ddot{\mathbf O}.
\]

The term \(-m_i\ddot{\mathbf O}\) is the pseudo-force due to using an accelerating origin. This point is important in the “origin choice” discussion below.

Split the force on particle \(i\) into external and internal parts:

\[
\mathbf F_i=\mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}+\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij},
\]

with \(\mathbf F_{ij}=-\mathbf F_{ji}\). For a rigid body one also assumes the internal forces are central, so that their net torque cancels.

\hrule

1. Translational Euler law

Sum Newton’s equations in an inertial frame:

\[
\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf R}_i
=
\sum_i \mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}
+
\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}.
\]

The internal forces cancel in pairs, so

\[
\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf R}_i=\mathbf F^{\rm ext}.
\]

Define total mass and center of mass by

\[
M=\sum_i m_i,\qquad
\mathbf R_G=\frac1M\sum_i m_i\mathbf R_i .
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
M\ddot{\mathbf R}_G=\mathbf F^{\rm ext}
}
\]

This is Euler’s first law, or the translational law for a rigid body: the center of mass moves as a point particle of mass \(M\) under the resultant external force.

If one uses a moving origin \(O\), define

\[
\mathbf r_G:=\mathbf R_G-\mathbf O.
\]

Then

\[
M\left(\ddot{\mathbf r}_G+\ddot{\mathbf O}\right)=\mathbf F^{\rm ext},
\]

or

\[
\boxed{
M\ddot{\mathbf r}_G=\mathbf F^{\rm ext}-M\ddot{\mathbf O}.
}
\]

Thus the center-of-mass law is independent of the origin only when written in an inertial frame. Relative to an accelerating origin, one must subtract the pseudo-force \(-M\ddot{\mathbf O}\).

\hrule

2. Angular momentum about an arbitrary point \(O\)

Define the angular momentum about \(O\) using relative velocities:

\[
\mathbf L_O
=
\sum_i m_i\,\mathbf r_i\times\dot{\mathbf r}_i,
\qquad
\mathbf r_i=\mathbf R_i-\mathbf O .
\]

Differentiate:

\[
\dot{\mathbf L}_O
=
\sum_i m_i\,\dot{\mathbf r}_i\times\dot{\mathbf r}_i
+
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i .
\]

The first term vanishes because \(\dot{\mathbf r}_i\times\dot{\mathbf r}_i=0\). Using the general relative-coordinate Newton equation,

\[
m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i
=
\mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}
+
\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}
-
m_i\ddot{\mathbf O},
\]

we get

\[
\dot{\mathbf L}_O
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times\mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}
+
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}
-
\left(\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i\right)\times\ddot{\mathbf O}.
\]

The internal torque cancels for central internal forces:

\[
\sum_{i<j}
(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j)\times\mathbf F_{ij}=0.
\]

Also

\[
\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i
=
M(\mathbf R_G-\mathbf O).
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf L}_O
=
\boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}
-
M(\mathbf R_G-\mathbf O)\times\ddot{\mathbf O}.
}
\]

where

\[
\boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}
=
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times\mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}
=
\sum_i (\mathbf R_i-\mathbf O)\times\mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}.
\]

This is the general rotational equation about a possibly moving origin \(O\).

Two very important special cases follow immediately.

Case A: \(O\) fixed in an inertial frame

Then \(\ddot{\mathbf O}=0\), so

\[
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf L}_O=\boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}.
}
\]

This is Euler’s second law about a fixed point.

Case B: \(O\) is the center of mass

Then \(\mathbf O=\mathbf R_G\), so \(\mathbf R_G-\mathbf O=0\). Hence

\[
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf L}_G=\boldsymbol\tau_G^{\rm ext}.
}
\]

Thus the rotational law also holds about the center of mass, even if the center of mass is accelerating. This is why rigid-body equations are usually written about the CM.

If one instead defines angular momentum using inertial velocities,

\[
\mathbf H_O=\sum_i(\mathbf R_i-\mathbf O)\times m_i\dot{\mathbf R}_i,
\]

then the corresponding formula is

\[
\dot{\mathbf H}_O
=
\boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}
-
\dot{\mathbf O}\times\mathbf P,
\qquad
\mathbf P=\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf R}_i=M\dot{\mathbf R}_G.
\]

Again the extra term vanishes if \(O\) is fixed or if \(O\) is the CM.

---

3. Rigid-body angular momentum and inertia tensor

Now choose \(O\) to be either

1. the center of mass \(G\), or
2. a point fixed in both the body and an inertial frame.

Then the position vectors \(\mathbf r_i\) relative to \(O\) are constant in a body-fixed frame. Let \(\boldsymbol\omega\) be the angular velocity of the rigid body. For a rigid body,

\[
\dot{\mathbf r}_i=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_i .
\]

Therefore

\[
\mathbf L_O
=
\sum_i m_i\,\mathbf r_i\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_i).
\]

Use

\[
\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)
=
\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)
-
\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),
\]

to obtain

\[
\mathbf L_O
=
\sum_i m_i
\left[
r_i^2\boldsymbol\omega
-
\mathbf r_i(\mathbf r_i\cdot\boldsymbol\omega)
\right].
\]

Define the inertia tensor about \(O\) by

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf I_O
=
\sum_i m_i
\left(
r_i^2\,\mathbf 1
-
\mathbf r_i\mathbf r_i^{\mathsf T}
\right),
}
\]

or in components,

\[
I_{O,ab}
=
\sum_i m_i
\left(
r_i^2\delta_{ab}
-
r_{ia}r_{ib}
\right).
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf L_O=\mathbf I_O\boldsymbol\omega .
}
\]

In a body-fixed frame, \(\mathbf I_O\) is constant. The rotational equation about \(O\) is therefore

\[
\boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}
=
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf I_O\boldsymbol\omega\right),
\]

valid when \(O\) is fixed in an inertial frame or is the center of mass.

---

4. Euler’s rotational equations

Let a superscript \(B\) denote components in the body-fixed frame. Since the body frame rotates with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\), the inertial time derivative of any vector \(\mathbf A\) has body-frame components

\[
\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{\!B}
=
\dot{\mathbf A}^{B}
+
\boldsymbol\omega^{B}\times\mathbf A^{B}.
\]

Apply this to \(\mathbf L_O=\mathbf I_O\boldsymbol\omega\). In the body frame, \(\mathbf I_O^{B}\) is constant, so

\[
\left(\frac{d\mathbf L_O}{dt}\right)^{B}
=
\mathbf I_O^{B}\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}^{B}
+
\boldsymbol\omega^{B}
\times
\left(\mathbf I_O^{B}\boldsymbol\omega^{B}\right).
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
\boldsymbol\tau_O^{B}
=
\mathbf I_O^{B}\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}^{B}
+
\boldsymbol\omega^{B}
\times
\left(\mathbf I_O^{B}\boldsymbol\omega^{B}\right).
}
\]

This is the vector form of Euler’s equations for a rigid body, about either the center of mass or a fixed point.

If the body axes are chosen as principal axes of inertia, then

\[
\mathbf I_O^{B}
=
\operatorname{diag}(I_1,I_2,I_3),
\]

and the equations become

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\tau_1 &= I_1\dot\omega_1-(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,\\[2mm]
\tau_2 &= I_2\dot\omega_2-(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,\\[2mm]
\tau_3 &= I_3\dot\omega_3-(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2.
\end{aligned}
}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tau_1 &= I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3,\\
\tau_2 &= I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1,\\
\tau_3 &= I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here \(I_1,I_2,I_3\) are the principal moments of inertia about \(O\), and \(\boldsymbol\tau_O\) is the external torque about \(O\).

---

5. Origin choice: summary

- Newton’s laws are simplest in an inertial frame. If \(\mathbf r_i=\mathbf R_i-\mathbf O\), then
  \[
  m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i
  \]
  only when \(\ddot{\mathbf O}=0\). For an accelerating origin,
  \[
  m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=\mathbf F_i-m_i\ddot{\mathbf O}.
  \]

- The translational law
  \[
  M\ddot{\mathbf R}_G=\mathbf F^{\rm ext}
  \]
  is independent of the choice of origin when written in an inertial frame.

- The rotational law
  \[
  \dot{\mathbf L}_O=\boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}
  \]
  holds cleanly when \(O\) is fixed in an inertial frame or when \(O\) is the center of mass. For an arbitrary moving origin,
  \[
  \dot{\mathbf L}_O
  =
  \boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}
  -
  M(\mathbf R_G-\mathbf O)\times\ddot{\mathbf O}.
  \]

- For Euler’s rigid-body equations
  \[
  \boldsymbol\tau_O
  =
  \mathbf I_O\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
  +
  \boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf I_O\boldsymbol\omega),
  \]
  one usually takes \(O=G\), the center of mass, or \(O\) a fixed pivot point. About the CM, translation and rotation decouple. About a fixed point, the point has zero acceleration, so the simple rotational equation remains valid, but the inertia tensor is taken about that fixed point.

Thus the two Euler laws are

\[
\boxed{
M\ddot{\mathbf R}_G=\mathbf F^{\rm ext},
}
\]

and, about the center of mass or a fixed point,

\[
\boxed{
\boldsymbol\tau_O^{\rm ext}
=
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf I_O\boldsymbol\omega\right)
=
\mathbf I_O\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
+
\boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf I_O\boldsymbol\omega).
}
\]

These follow directly from Newton’s second law for the individual particles, with the internal forces cancelling in both the resultant force and the resultant torque.
